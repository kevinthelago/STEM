Think of a matrix \(A\) as a transformation of space. Most vectors get knocked off their original line when you apply \(A\). The eigenvectors are the rare directions that survive — \(A\) only stretches or squashes them: \[ A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} \] The scalar \(\lambda\) is the eigenvalue — exactly how much that surviving direction is scaled.
Visualize: eigenvectors of a 2×2 shear <viz type="eigen-2d"> · opens Visualize host
So how do I actually find \(\lambda\) for a given matrix?
You
Tutor streaming
We want a non-zero \(\mathbf{v}\) with \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\). That can only happen if \(A-\lambda I\) collapses space — i.e. it's singular — so we solve the characteristic equation: \[ \det(A-\lambda I)=0 \] For a 2×2 matrix this is just a quadratic in \(\lambda\)

Implement power_iteration(A, n) returning the dominant eigenvector.
def power_iteration(A, n):
v = np.random.rand(A.shape[0])
for _ in range(n):
v = A @ v
v = v / np.linalg.norm(v)
return v
